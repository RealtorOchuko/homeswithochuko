Most buyers I talk to this time of year ask the same thing: should we wait until spring? It's a fair question, especially with mortgage rates where they are. Freddie Mac's weekly survey for November 12, 2026 put the average 30-year fixed rate at 7.11% and the 15-year at 6.45%. That's down from 7.18% the week before, but well above the 6.24% average from a year earlier.



Higher rates make the monthly payment harder to swallow. They also change how the negotiation goes. In my experience, late fall and winter are when Bay Area buyers who are ready have the most room to ask for terms that would be hard to get in April. Here's how I'd think about it.



\## Why winter can work in a buyer's favor



Sellers who list between Thanksgiving and early February usually have a reason to. They may have a job move or a home they've already bought. Or their listing may have carried over from the fall without selling. Fewer people are touring on rainy Saturdays, so there's often less pressure to waive everything just to stay in the running.



None of that means prices fall in the winter. Desirable homes in Fremont, San Jose and on the Peninsula can still draw several offers in any month. What changes more often is how willing a seller is to give on terms. Over the life of a loan, terms can matter as much as the price.



\## Ask for credits, not just a lower price



When rates are above 7%, a lower price is not always the best thing to ask for. A seller credit can sometimes do more for your monthly budget. Some common ways buyers use one:



\- \*\*Rate buydown.\*\* The credit goes to your lender to lower your rate. A temporary buydown lowers it for the first year or two, and a permanent buydown pays points to lower it for the full loan term.

\- \*\*Closing costs.\*\* This keeps more of your cash available for reserves or move-in work.

\- \*\*Repair credit.\*\* Instead of asking the seller to hire someone, you get money at closing to fix inspection items with your own contractor.



Lenders limit how large a seller credit can be, and the limit depends on the loan type and your down payment. Talk to your lender before you write an offer so the number you ask for can actually be used. The right structure depends on how long you plan to keep the loan. If you might refinance, ask your lender to run the break-even math on a permanent buydown against a temporary one.



\## Rainy season is the best time to inspect



This is the part I look at as a former builder. Rain shows you what dry months hide. If you're touring and inspecting in winter, use that.



\- \*\*Roof and attic.\*\* Look for staining on the roof sheathing or around vents and chimneys. If you can, go up into the attic after a storm. Fresh water stains tell you more than any seller disclosure.

\- \*\*Grading and drainage.\*\* Watch where water goes during or right after rain. Soil should slope away from the foundation. Puddles against the house, or downspouts emptying right at the foundation, are early warnings.

\- \*\*Crawl spaces and slabs.\*\* Older homes in Oakland, San Jose and the Peninsula often have raised foundations. Standing water, efflorescence (white mineral deposits) on the stem walls, or a musty smell in the crawl space are worth a closer look by a foundation or drainage specialist.

\- \*\*Windows and doors.\*\* Sticking doors and damp sills can point to settling or to failed flashing.



On homes built before the 1980s, I also suggest a sewer lateral inspection. Many Bay Area cities have their own sewer lateral rules, and replacing a line is a real expense you'd want to know about before you remove contingencies.



The point of all this is not to scare you off a house. It's to turn inspection findings into numbers you can negotiate with. "The roof is old" is easy for a seller to brush off. A written roofing bid is much harder to ignore.



\## Keep contingencies that protect you



In busy spring markets, buyers sometimes feel they have to waive inspection and appraisal contingencies to compete. In a slower month, you may be able to keep them, or at least shorten them instead of dropping them. Some approaches that keep an offer strong:



\- Do your inspections before you write the offer, so your inspection contingency can be short.

\- Get fully underwritten by your lender, not just pre-approved, so the seller sees less financing risk.

\- Offer a closing date that fits the seller's timeline.



Every situation is different, and what you can safely waive depends on your finances. For questions about loan structure, talk with your lender. For contract questions, a real estate attorney can help.



\## Run the numbers both ways



Before deciding to wait, compare two scenarios with your lender: buying now with whatever credits you can negotiate, and buying later at whatever rate and price conditions turn out to be. Nobody can promise where rates or prices go next. What you can control is a payment you're comfortable with, a home you've inspected well, and terms that leave you with cash for reserves.
