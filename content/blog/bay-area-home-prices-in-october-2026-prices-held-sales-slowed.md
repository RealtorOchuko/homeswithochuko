The California Association of REALTORS® released its October 2026 numbers this morning, and the Bay Area story has two parts: prices held up, but fewer homes sold. If you own a home in San Francisco, on the Peninsula, in the East Bay or in San Jose, both parts matter to you. 

Below is what the data shows for each county, and what I'd do with it if I were selling this winter or just keeping an eye on my home's value. 

The big picture: fewer sales, steady prices. 

According to C.A.R.'s October 2026 report, the median price of an existing single-family home in the nine-county Bay Area was $1,375,000. That's up about 5.8% from October 2025 and up 3.8% from September. Sales went the other way: down about 12% from a year ago, though slightly up from September. 

Most of that comes down to mortgage rates. C.A.R. puts the average 30-year fixed rate at 7.33% in October, up from 6.86% in September and 6.25% a year earlier. When rates jumped in late September, many buyers paused to rework their budgets. The buyers who stayed in the market were still willing to pay for the right home. 

Statewide, sales fell to their slowest pace in nearly three years, while the median price was about flat year over year at roughly $887,000. The Bay Area was the only region in the state where sales rose from September to October.

County by county. 

All figures below are C.A.R.'s October 2026 medians for existing single-family homes.

\- \*\*San Francisco:\*\* $2,390,000, up about 29% year over year. Homes took a median of 26.5 days to sell, and the county had only about 1.2 months of inventory.

\- \*\*San Mateo (Peninsula):\*\* $2,385,000, up about 9%. Homes sold in a median of 11 days, with 1.8 months of supply.

\- \*\*Santa Clara (San Jose):\*\* about $2.06 million, up about 6%. Homes still sold quickly, in a median of 11 days, but inventory is changing fast. Active listings were up nearly 44% from a year ago, and months of supply rose to 2.1 from 1.5.

\- \*\*Alameda (Oakland, Fremont, Newark):\*\* $1,278,000, essentially flat (+0.2%). Homes sold in a median of 14 days, but sales fell about 19.5% from last October. That was the steepest drop of the counties listed here. 

A word of caution on that San Francisco jump. A median is simply the middle sale among whatever homes happened to close that month. When more high-end homes close and fewer entry-level ones do, the median rises even if no individual house gained 29%. C.A.R. makes the same point in its report: a change in the median reflects the mix of homes sold, not the change in value of any particular house. The number does say San Francisco demand is strong, but I wouldn't use it to reprice your home.

What this means if you're selling

At about 2.4 months of supply, the Bay Area still has the tightest inventory in California, compared with 3.8 months statewide. Sellers still have the edge here. But fewer buyers are writing offers, and the ones who are have run their numbers at rates above 7%. Here's what I'm telling sellers right now:

\- \*\*Price to the comps, not to the headline.\*\* A county median won't tell you what your street will bear. Recent sales of similar homes nearby will.

\- \*\*Expect buyers to read the disclosures closely.\*\* With fewer offers per home, buyers have more room to negotiate on repairs.

\- \*\*In Santa Clara County especially, you'll be competing with more listings.\*\* With listings up sharply from last year, presentation and pricing matter more than they did in spring.

\- \*\*Winter isn't a bad time to list.\*\* Buyers shopping in November and December tend to be serious, and fewer other homes are on the market.

The builder's eye: fix what buyers will find in the first rain. 

Before real estate, I spent years in residential construction and pool building. That work taught me that a house shows its real condition in the first big storms of the season. In a slower market, the homes that sit unsold are usually the ones where a buyer's inspector found a problem the seller never fixed. 

Before you list this winter, walk your property after a good rain:

\- Check gutters and downspouts. They should carry water well away from the foundation, not let it pool against it.

\- Look for fresh stains on ceilings below roof valleys and around skylights.

\- Check the crawlspace, or the edges of the garage slab, for standing water or a white, chalky residue on the concrete. Both point to moisture.

\- If you have a pool, look at the deck joints and the coping. Water that gets under the deck causes cracking and movement over time. 

Most of these are inexpensive to fix now. If a buyer's inspector finds them during escrow, they become credit requests that cost far more than the repair would have. A pre-listing inspection is often money well spent, and I'm always glad to walk a home with a seller before it goes on the market.

What I'm watching next 

C.A.R. notes that rates have been trending down in recent weeks. Its chief economist also expects pent-up demand to keep building while buyers wait on the sidelines. If rates ease into early 2027, I'd expect some of those buyers to return quickly, especially in the tighter San Francisco and Peninsula markets. In Santa Clara County, I'm watching whether the jump in listings starts to show up in prices. 

None of this replaces a close look at your own home. County numbers set the backdrop, but your block, your floor plan and your home's condition decide the price.
